Common misconceptions of nuclear power

1. A nuclear reactor explode like a nuclear bomb

No. A nuclear reactor does not have the right density of fissionable materials within the core for it to explode like a nuclear weapon. Nuclear weapons typically has a nuclear warhead consisting of above 90% fissile material, meaning a chain reaction happens a lot faster due to the high density of fissile material in comparison to a nuclear reactor.

2. Commmerical nuclear power plant technology can be used to proliferate nuclear weapons

Nuclear weapons require enriched uranium or plutonium to such a higher degree than that needed for a nuclear reactor. Therefore, the use of a nuclear reactor does not necessarily mean that a country could produce nuclear weapons. However, if a country has the technology and infrastructure to enrich its own nuclear fuel, then a country could rapidly produce a nuclear weapon that can be used. However, the United Nations hold their own inspections into countries which enrich their own nuclear fuels through the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). If countries are found to be breaking these international laws or not co-operating with IAEA inspectors, then that country is likely to face sanctions. An example is Iran in 2025 were subject to sanctions due to the IAEA finding that Iran broke their obligations to permit IAEA inspectors to inspect their nuclear program.

3. Nuclear power plants give off a lot of radiation to the local enviroment

Exposure to nuclear radiation increases the likelihood of cancer because radiation can alter DNA molecules within the body. In addition, high level exposure, can cause organ damage and skin burns. For this reason nuclear powerplants aim to reduce as much exposure and leaks of nuclear radiation. The internationally accepted safety limit on nuclear radiation dose is 1 millisievert (mSv) per person per year. Usually a person living near a nuclear power plant is exposed to roughly 0.0001 mSv per year, which is a tiny fraction of the international safety limit. For some perspective, you consume 0.29 mSv per year on average from just food.

4. The nuclear power industry does not have a solution to the nuclear waste problem

Nuclear waste is an umbrella term, which includes everything from specialised clothing worn by workers who are exposed to above-average radiation to the spent fuel from the core. Nuclear waste is categorised into three different levels according to their radioactivity: low, intermediate and high level. Low level waste includes any clothing or tools used in areas with a higher background radiation than is average. Low level waste accounts for roughly 90% of the total volume of nuclear waste, whilst this only contains around 1% of the radioactivity in nuclear waste. This low level waste is disposed of in near-surface disposal sites, where the waste is put into special containers and put into vaults such that the radiation is contained.





For high level waste, which accounts for roughly 3% of total volume of waste, it is either recycled or buried deep underground. Approximately 97% of used fuel can be recycled and reused in a nuclear reactor. To recycle the used fuel, the left over uranium and plutonium found in the fuel rods are separated from the rods. The left over fuel rods are then buried deep underground in containers that leak as little nuclear radiation as possible.

Due to a large amount of spent fuel is able to be recycled and that the majority of nuclear waste is considered low level, then there does not seem to be a large issue around nuclear waste. Nuclear waste is disposed of in regions which cause the least impact on the environment as possible.

5. Nuclear energy is the answer to the climate crisis

Many politicians and political commentators suggest that nuclear energy is the answer to the climate crisis since it is: a low-carbon energy source, it operates 24/7 (unlike solar or wind) and much of the infrastructure from fossil fuel energy production can be repurposed for nuclear energy. However, there are a few issues with the claim that nuclear energy is the answer to the climate crisis. Firstly, nuclear power plants take years to establish due in part to all the safety features involved in nuclear energy production. This would mean that we could not as quickly reorganise our energy production.

Also for the world to combat climate change we would need a global consensus. Since nuclear technology has been for year guarded by nuclear powers, such as the UK, they are unlikely to help out countries to convert to nuclear power, since this could lead to an increase in the total number of nuclear weapons. Without a global conversion to non-greenhouse emitting energy production, then any effort to combat climate change would virtually impossible.

In conclusion, nuclear power is not the sol answer to the climate crisis, however it is important to recognise the real positives that come along with nuclear power, the main one being 24/7 energy production with little carbon dioxide emissions.
