Understanding 5G

Before exploring the misinformation, learn what 5G actually is and why it matters.

It is the newest generation of cellular network technology. Its primary purpose is data transmission over a network, with each generation having increasing speed of transmission, a higher capacity for the amount of data sent, smaller delay, and improved efficiency.

It allows a huge number of devices to connect together to communicate over a network. You may have seen the 5G symbol on your mobile phone before, and this means your phone is connected to a nearby 5G base station and is using 5G radio waves to transmit and receive data. This could be for activities such as video streaming or web browsing.

As the number of devices connected to the internet and the demand for data sharing increases, 5G is evermore important.

With the recent advancements in the use of Artificial Intelligence, which rely on internet access, as well as the exponential growth of global data usage, the fast speeds and high capacities of 5G are vital for maintaining systems.

Some new technologies such as remote surgeries, home security, and autonomous vehicles use 5G, and so the slower lag time (also known as latency) it provides in comparison with its predecessors is crucial in these circumstances to ensure safety.

5G works by using radio frequencies ranging from 20KHz to 6GHz. The geographical map is split into different cells, each with a 5G base station (or 5G cellular tower). A 5G wireless device in that cell connects to the base station using radio waves.

This base station can then route data across the internet or to other devices.

5G uses many of the same radio frequencies as 3G and 4G technologies. However, it uses smaller, lower power transmitters than 4G so that they can be placed easily in many areas. This improves speed, capacity, and delay of a network.

Frequencies used by 5G
5G generally uses frequencies from 20KHz to tens of GHz. Some studies quote that 5G uses frequencies up to 300GHz, however this is the theoretical upper bound, used as a safety limit for future research. 300GHz represents the upper bound of the radiofrequency category, and is simply used as a limit to divide the difference between electromagnetic waves. In reality the maximum frequencies 5G uses is no greater than in the 10s of GHz at its peak. These are what's known as millimetre waves used for very high speed transmissions over very short distances, in order to speed up transmission.

Health Effects
All frequencies used by 5G are in the non-ionising part of the electromagnetic spectrum since they have low frequencies. This means they cannot damage living tissue or mutate DNA. This is unlike ionising radiation which has much higher frequencies and can cause cancer.
