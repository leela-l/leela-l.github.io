What is Climate Change?

Climate change is the long-term change in the Earth’s weather and temperature conditions. Over the past one hundred years the world has been warming up and a result weather patterns are shifting.

Understanding Climate Change

Lets learn the science behind climate change and how it actually works

In the Earth’s atmosphere there are gases, such as carbon dioxide, water vapour and methane, that are known as greenhouse gases.

Solar radiation, mostly visible light, passes through the atmosphere and heats up the Earth’s surface.

The surface then emits energy back upwards as infrared radiation.

Some of the heat escapes back into space, but the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere act as a blanket, trapping some of the escaping heat.

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth about 33°c warmer than it otherwise would be. Without it the planet would be frozen.

Climate Feedback Loops

The warming of the Earth is not just a straight-line warming. It happens as a sequence of interactions in the climate system called a climate feedback loop. A positive feedback loop amplifies the original change, while a negative feedback loop dampens it.
Its important to note that “positive” doesn’t mean good. It just means it boosts the system.

Positive Feedback Loops

Ice-Albedo Feedback: Ice and snow reflect 60-90% of sunlight. As the ice melts the ocean and land absorb much more solar radiation, leading to further warming and ice melting, creating a self-reinforcing cycle. This is why the Arctic is warming three times faster than the global average.

Water Vapour: Warmer air holds more water vapor. Carbon dioxide causes the temperature to rise, so the air holds more water. The water vapor is a greenhouse gas and traps more heat, this increases the warming even more. It is important to note water vapor is not the initial driver, it acts as a feedback responding to the temperature. This effect roughly doubles the warming caused by carbon dioxide alone.

Permafrost Thawing: In Arctic regions there are areas of permafrost. They form when the ground temperature stays below freezing for two or more years and water trapped in the soil freezes. The active upper layer thaws seasonally. When this happens microbes decompose organic matter and carbon dioxide and methane are released into the atmosphere. This is concerning because permafrost stores about twice as much carbon as currently in the atmosphere, and methane can be up to 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide in the first 20 years after its release. While this feedback loop is slower it is potentially long-lasting.

Negative Feedback Loops

Planck Feedback: As the Earth gets warmer, it radiates more energy back to space in the form of outgoing infrared radiation. This partially offsets the warming and is the strongest stabilizing feedback. It is named after Max Planck as it comes directly from black body radiation physics.
An object above absolute zero emits radiation. The amount emitted depends heavily on temperature as stated by the Stefan-Boltzmann law E = σT4 (E is energy, σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant and T is temperature). Small increases in temperature cause larger increases in emitted radiation.

Fertilization effect: Higher CO2 can stimulate plant growth and more plants means more carbon uptake. But this process is limited by nutrients and water and is not strong enough to cancel emissions.
