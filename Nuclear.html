Nuclear Energy: introduction

Civil nuclear energy production has been on the decline throughout Europe, with countries like Germany completely phasing out their nuclear reactors in 2023. However, recently in the UK, the government has started to look into building new, smaller nuclear reactors across the country. In addition, France has scrapped their previous aim to reduce nuclear energy reliance from 70% of their energy production to 50% in 2023. So the question is, why are European countries so divided over nuclear energy?

One of the biggest issues Nuclear energy has faced is public perception. The word nuclear has become interlinked with apocalyptic explosions or dangerous green radiation, as seen in The Simpsons. In the UK, 41% of the population opposes local nuclear power plants, whilst only 21% support local nuclear power plants. The primary reason for opposition was due to concerns about the safety and security of the local area, with 80% of those opposed listing this as a primary factor. Others focused their concerns on the impact on local plant and animal life.

With the UK aiming to become carbon-neutral by 2050, the Government have been reevaluating nuclear energy, with the government set to increase electricity production by building new nuclear power plants. The plan is for the new nuclear power plants to produce 24GW by 2050, which could provide for up to a quarter of the UK’s projected electricity demands.

With the UK government looking to expand nuclear energy, it is important for us to gain a better understanding of nuclear energy. Below we outline some commonly asked questions and address misunderstandings that arise from incorrect use of physics.

Common misconceptions of nuclear power

1. A nuclear reactor explode like a nuclear bomb

No. A nuclear reactor does not have the right density of fissionable materials within the core for it to explode like a nuclear weapon. Nuclear weapons typically has a nuclear warhead consisting of above 90% fissile material, meaning a chain reaction happens a lot faster due to the high density of fissile material in comparison to a nuclear reactor.

2. Commmerical nuclear power plant technology can be used to proliferate nuclear weapons

Nuclear weapons require enriched uranium or plutonium to such a higher degree than that needed for a nuclear reactor. Therefore, the use of a nuclear reactor does not necessarily mean that a country could produce nuclear weapons. However, if a country has the technology and infrastructure to enrich its own nuclear fuel, then a country could rapidly produce a nuclear weapon that can be used. However, the United Nations hold their own inspections into countries which enrich their own nuclear fuels through the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). If countries are found to be breaking these international laws or not co-operating with IAEA inspectors, then that country is likely to face sanctions. An example is Iran in 2025 were subject to sanctions due to the IAEA finding that Iran broke their obligations to permit IAEA inspectors to inspect their nuclear program.
