Basics of nuclear reactors

All current commercial nuclear power plants use a process called nuclear fission to produce large amounts of energy. Nuclear fission is a process where large, unstable atoms split into two or more smaller atoms. Below is a simulation of nuclear fission.

Usually, nuclear fission inside a nuclear reactor will use nuclear fuel consisting of the element Uranium-235, which is an isotope of the element uranium-238. An isotope describes an atom’s nucleus, which has a different number of neutrons than its more common elemental form. In this case, Uranium-235 has 3 fewer neutrons than the element Uranium, making the atom unstable, wanting to split apart.

If a Uranium-235 nucleus gets hit by a neutron, the Uranium-235 nucleus becomes Uranium-236, creating an incredibly unstable nucleus, which then splits into two new nuclei and 3 neutrons, as shown in the diagram. During this process, a large amount of energy is outputted in the form of heat.

In a nuclear reactor, neutrons are launched at Uranium-235 to force nuclear fission, which creates large amounts of heat energy. From here, the heat energy is used to boil water into steam, which is used to turn a turbine, generating electricity.
